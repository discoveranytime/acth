ACTH 1-39
the stress signal

A 39-amino-acid messenger from your pituitary gland that orders your adrenal glands to make cortisol. As Acthar Gel it is an FDA-approved prescription medicine with 19 labeled uses — and it is also the basis of the standard test for adrenal failure.

Quick read

ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) is made in the front of the pituitary gland and tells the adrenal cortex to release cortisol, the body's main stress steroid. The drug version, Acthar Gel, has been FDA-approved since 1952 for conditions including infantile spasms, MS relapses, and nephrotic syndrome. A short synthetic cousin, cosyntropin (ACTH 1-24), is what doctors inject to test whether your adrenals still answer the call.

01The signal

What ACTH 1-39 actually is

ACTH is one of the body's original group chats: a short peptide message, sent from the brain's pituitary gland, that tells the adrenal glands sitting on your kidneys to make cortisol now. The '1-39' just means it is the full-length, 39-amino-acid version.

Made in the anterior pituitary · orders cortisol from the adrenals

A pituitary messenger

ACTH is carved out of a larger parent protein called POMC inside the anterior pituitary. When the brain senses stress, inflammation, or low cortisol, ACTH is released into the bloodstream within minutes.

Cortisol is the reply

ACTH docks onto melanocortin-2 receptors on the adrenal cortex. The adrenals answer by manufacturing cortisol — the steroid that raises blood sugar, damps inflammation, and helps you handle stress.

The drug is a preparation

Acthar Gel is a purified pituitary-gland preparation containing ACTH activity, suspended in gel for slow release. It is a prescription medicine, not a supplement, and not the same as any single synthetic peptide.

Plain English

Think of ACTH as a text message from mission control (the pituitary) to the factory floor (the adrenals): 'we need cortisol, stat.' The factory ramps up production, and when cortisol levels rise high enough, they text back 'we're good' — the feedback loop that keeps the whole system balanced.

02History

From stress research to 1952 approval

ACTH was one of the first hormones medicine learned to bottle. Its story runs from mid-century stress science to a modern drug with one of the longest labels in the pharmacy.

Seven decades from lab curiosity to a 19-indication prescription drug.
A very old approval

Acthar Gel was approved in 1952 — before modern trial standards existed. Many of its 19 indications were grandfathered in from an era of looser evidence rules, which is why the label reads like a history book.

The diagnostic cousin

Cosyntropin — synthetic ACTH 1-24, just the first 24 amino acids — is all you need to fully stimulate the adrenal receptor. Doctors inject it and measure cortisol to check for adrenal insufficiency.

Still in use

Unlike most 1950s drugs, ACTH never left the clinic. It remains a first-line option for infantile spasms and a fallback for MS relapses when steroids fail or can't be used.

Plain English

ACTH is a veteran — approved when medicine was younger and kept on because, for a handful of serious conditions, it genuinely works. Its long label is part proof of usefulness, part museum of old approvals.

03The HPA axis

How the command chain works

ACTH sits in the middle of the HPA axis — hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal — the body's central stress thermostat. Here is the loop, step by step.

The command chain, in order: CRH from the hypothalamus → ACTH from the anterior pituitary → cortisol from the adrenal cortex. The dots show the direction of travel.
ACTH's main target is the thick middle layer — the zona fasciculata — where cortisol is made. Aldosterone comes from the outer zone and answers to a different system (renin-angiotensin + potassium).
The Cortisol Factory: ACTH docks on membrane-bound MC2R and never enters the cell. Inside, Gs → cAMP → PKA moves cholesterol into the mitochondria, where steroidogenic enzymes build cortisol.
The stand-down: rising cortisol travels the bloodstream back to the brain and tells the pituitary and hypothalamus to ease off. Feedback runs opposite to the command signal — that is what makes the loop a thermostat.
Cortisol release after ACTHreliable & fast
Healthy adrenals answer an ACTH signal with a cortisol surge within 30–60 minutes — the basis of the stimulation test.
Feedback shutdown of ACTHtextbook physiology
Rising cortisol suppresses further ACTH release — the thermostat behavior every endocrinology student learns.
Anti-inflammatory effectwell established
Much of ACTH's medical effect rides on the cortisol it triggers, plus direct melanocortin-receptor signaling on immune cells.
Effect beyond cortisolactive research
Acthar Gel also stimulates other melanocortin receptors, which may explain effects that plain steroids don't reproduce.
Plain English

The loop is a thermostat: cold room → heater on (ACTH) → room warms (cortisol) → thermostat clicks off. Break any link — a sluggish pituitary, exhausted adrenals — and the whole house feels it.

04Evidence

What the science actually supports

Unlike most peptides on this site, ACTH has decades of human data — because it is an approved medicine. The evidence is strongest in a few sharp indications and thinner everywhere else.

Infantile spasms: strong

In the key randomized trial, 87% of infants on Acthar Gel had spasms stop and brain-wave patterns normalize, versus 29% on prednisone. It remains a first-line therapy for children under 2.

MS relapses: speeds recovery

Controlled trials show it speeds resolution of acute MS exacerbations — but the label is explicit: no evidence it changes the disease's ultimate course.

The long tail: label-based

Nephrotic syndrome, sarcoidosis, lupus, eye inflammation and more sit on the label from older, smaller studies. Used, but with less modern proof than the headline indications.

✅ What ACTH does

Tells healthy adrenals to make cortisol on demand — the core of normal stress physiology. Drives the ACTH stimulation test: a diagnostic that checks whether adrenals can answer the call. Works through MC2R on the adrenal cortex, a target no other hormone owns. Helps in specific inflammatory and neurological conditions where its label indications sit.

❌ What it doesn't do

It does not directly make cortisol appear — the adrenal factory does that. It does not replace cortisol itself; the two are different medicines for different jobs. It does not fix a broken adrenal gland — if the factory is damaged, shouting louder at it changes nothing. It does not work as a general wellness or energy signal outside diagnosed disease.

🧪 Clinical diagnostic relevance

The short ACTH fragment cosyntropin (1-24) is the standard stimulation test for adrenal failure: inject it, measure cortisol at 30–60 minutes, and you learn whether the adrenals can respond. A flat cortisol curve points to primary adrenal insufficiency; measuring natural ACTH alongside cortisol separates pituitary problems from adrenal problems. This is one of the most-used hormone tests in medicine.

Evidence limitations

Two indications (infantile spasms, MS relapses) have modern randomized trials. The rest of the 19 sit on older, smaller studies by 1950s–80s standards — used, but with thinner proof than today's regulators would demand. Long-term course data (years of repeated therapy) is sparse. Anecdotes — including community reports — are not substitutes for that missing evidence.

Plain English

Picture a report card with two A's, several B's, and a long list of 'present but ungraded.' For infantile spasms and MS flares the record is solid; for the rest of the 19 indications, doctors lean on history, small studies, and clinical judgment.

06ACTH vs the field

ACTH vs its closest relatives

ACTH gets confused with its neighbors. Here is exactly where each hormone operates in the HPA axis — same family, different jobs.

Where each hormone sits in the chain. The comparisons below start from this map.

ACTH vs CRH

CRH is the hypothalamus's whisper to the pituitary — step one, acting locally on corticotroph cells. ACTH is the pituitary's broadcast to the adrenals — step two, traveling the bloodstream. Both get quieted by cortisol feedback, but they never swap jobs.

ACTH vs Cortisol

ACTH is the order slip; cortisol is the delivered product. ACTH works upstream (brain → adrenal); cortisol works everywhere downstream (metabolism, immunity, brain). In the clinic they are different tools: ACTH tests or drives the factory, cortisol replaces the product.

ACTH vs α-MSH

Both are cut from the same POMC parent protein and both are melanocortins — but the locks differ. MC2R is uniquely specific for ACTH and lives mostly in the adrenal cortex. α-MSH works through other melanocortin receptors (pigmentation, appetite, energy). Same family, different doors.

One parent protein, two very different messengers. MC2R answers only to ACTH.
Plain English

Think of a restaurant: CRH is the host seating you, ACTH is the waiter carrying your order to the kitchen, and cortisol is the food arriving at the table. α-MSH is a different waiter from the same training school, serving a different restaurant entirely.

08Who it may suit

Who it may suit — and who it may not

Nineteen labeled indications — and a firm line between prescribed medicine and internet experimentation.

Who it may be relevant to

  • Infants with infantile spasms — under specialist care, where the trial evidence is strongest.
  • People with MS relapses or labeled inflammatory conditions where a clinician prescribes it.
  • Patients being evaluated for adrenal insufficiency — via the cosyntropin stimulation test, a standard diagnostic.

Who it may not suit — or needs extra caution

  • Anyone seeking a general energy, stress, or “recomp” hack — not an indication; HPA-axis disruption is the price.
  • People with uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, or active infections — label cautions apply.
  • Pregnancy without specialist input — data is thin; the label flags caution.
  • Anyone considering gray-market supply — no monitoring, no supervision, no equivalence to the medicine.
A clear line

This is general information, not personal medical advice. Dosing, monitoring, and stopping are all clinician decisions — the gray-market world offers none of them.

09Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

An approved drug means a real label with real warnings. ACTH's risks are mostly the familiar price of pushing the cortisol system hard.

Known

Steroid-like effects

On the label: fluid retention, high blood pressure, blood-sugar spikes, weight gain, mood changes, and suppressed immunity. Push cortisol up and the body pays cortisol's bills.

Known

Adrenal suppression

On the label: after prolonged stimulation, your own HPA axis can go quiet. Stopping suddenly can leave you cortisol-deficient until the loop reboots — tapering is a medical decision.

Known

Infection vulnerability

On the label: dampened inflammation means dampened defense. Latent infections can flare, and new infections can hit harder while on therapy.

Theoretical

Long-term unknowns

Theoretical: most modern data covers short courses. What repeated or prolonged courses do over years — bones, metabolism, eyes — is less well mapped than the acute effects.

Unknown

Rare populations

Unknown: data in pregnancy, frail elderly patients, and people with multiple interacting conditions is thin. The label flags caution; specifics are sparse.

Known

Not a DIY drug

Observed fact: this is prescription-only for a reason — dosing, monitoring (blood pressure, glucose, electrolytes), and stopping all need a clinician. The gray-market peptide world has no equivalent of any of this.

📈 Too much ACTH: Cushing physiology

When ACTH runs high for a long time — a pituitary tumor, or chronic overstimulation — the adrenals pour out cortisol without a stand-down. The result is Cushing physiology: central weight gain, fragile skin, high blood pressure, blood-sugar spikes, and bone thinning. The same thermostat, stuck in the on position. This is physiology, not a recommendation — it is why excess signaling is a disease, not a hack.

📉 Too little: adrenal insufficiency

When the loop breaks — damaged adrenals, or a pituitary that stops sending ACTH — cortisol runs too low. The result is adrenal insufficiency: crushing fatigue, low blood pressure, salt craving, and danger in a crisis (adrenal crisis is a medical emergency). After prolonged ACTH therapy, the body's own loop can go quiet — which is exactly why stopping is a clinician's call, never a solo project.

Plain English

ACTH's deal is honest: real power, real side effects, real supervision. It borrows trouble from the same place it borrows benefit — the cortisol system — and the label tells you so up front.

10Glossary

Understanding ACTH Terminology

Twelve terms, each with the scientific definition and a plain-English translation. Tap any term to expand it.

The scienceAdrenocorticotropic hormone, a 39-amino-acid peptide secreted by the anterior pituitary gland in response to stress and to CRH from the hypothalamus. Its primary job is to stimulate the adrenal cortex — via the MC2R melanocortin receptor — to produce cortisol and other steroid hormones. As a medicine (notably Acthar Gel), it is used for infantile spasms, multiple sclerosis flares, nephrotic syndrome, and other conditions where stimulating the body's own steroid production is preferred. Its release follows a daily rhythm, peaking in the early morning. It is the middle link of the HPA axis: the pituitary's order to the adrenals.

Plain EnglishACTH is the pituitary gland's "make stress hormone now" signal — a 39-piece peptide sent to the adrenal glands sitting atop the kidneys. Its main job is ordering the adrenals to produce cortisol, the body's core stress steroid. As a medicine it is used for infantile seizures, MS flares, and certain kidney disorders, by pushing the body to make its own steroids. Its levels rise and fall daily, peaking in the early morning. Think of it as the middle manager of the stress system: the brain tells the pituitary, the pituitary tells the adrenals.

The scienceThe principal glucocorticoid steroid hormone produced by the adrenal cortex under ACTH's direction. It raises blood sugar by promoting glucose release from the liver, suppresses inflammation and immune activity, and helps the body mobilize energy during stress. Its release follows a circadian rhythm — highest in the morning, lowest at night — and is tightly regulated by the HPA axis feedback loop. Chronically high cortisol, whether from disease or medication, causes weight gain, bone loss, and immune suppression; too little causes adrenal crisis. It is essential for life, but only in the right amount at the right time.

Plain EnglishCortisol is the body's main stress steroid, made by the adrenal glands on ACTH's orders. It raises blood sugar for quick energy, damps down inflammation, and helps you respond to emergencies. It runs on a daily clock — highest in the morning, lowest at night — and the brain keeps it in range with a feedback loop. Too much for too long brings weight gain, fragile bones, and a weakened immune system; too little can trigger a medical emergency. You cannot live without it, but the dose and the timing have to be right.

The scienceThe hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the three-gland control system governing the stress response and cortisol production. The hypothalamus releases CRH, which tells the anterior pituitary to release ACTH, which tells the adrenal cortex to make cortisol; rising cortisol then signals the brain to stand down, closing the feedback loop. This cascade also follows a daily rhythm and responds within minutes to physical or psychological stress. Dysregulation of the axis is implicated in Cushing's syndrome (too much cortisol), Addison's disease (too little), and chronic stress disorders. It is one of the body's master regulatory circuits.

Plain EnglishThe HPA axis is the stress command chain: three glands in sequence — the hypothalamus, the pituitary, and the adrenals. The hypothalamus nudges the pituitary, the pituitary sends ACTH, and the adrenals answer with cortisol; then rising cortisol tells the brain to ease off, closing the loop. The whole chain reacts within minutes to stress and also ticks along on a daily rhythm. When it misfires, you get diseases of too much cortisol or too little. It is one of the body's master control circuits, and ACTH is its middle link.

The sciencePro-opiomelanocortin, a large precursor protein made in the anterior pituitary (and in smaller amounts elsewhere, including the skin and immune cells). Enzymes cleave it into several active peptides: ACTH, plus melanocyte-stimulating hormones, beta-endorphin, and beta-lipotropin, among others. Which fragments are produced depends on the tissue doing the cutting — the pituitary mainly yields ACTH, while the brain's arcuate nucleus favors the melanocortins and endorphins. This one-protein-many-products design explains why ACTH, skin pigmentation signals, and natural opioids share a common origin. POMC is the parent molecule of an entire signaling family.

Plain EnglishPOMC is one big parent protein that gets sliced into several different messengers — ACTH is just the most famous slice. The same protein also yields melanocyte-stimulating hormones, the natural opioid beta-endorphin, and a few others. Which pieces get cut out depends on the tissue: the pituitary mostly makes ACTH from it, while parts of the brain favor the other fragments. That shared origin is why stress hormones, skin-tanning signals, and natural painkillers are all chemical cousins. One protein, a whole family of signals.

The scienceA family of five G-protein-coupled receptors (MC1R through MC5R) that respond to ACTH and the melanocyte-stimulating hormones cut from POMC. MC2R, found almost exclusively in the adrenal cortex, is the ACTH receptor that triggers cortisol production — and the only melanocortin receptor that responds to ACTH alone. The others mediate different effects: MC1R controls skin and hair pigmentation, MC4R regulates appetite and energy balance, and MC3R and MC5R have roles in inflammation and gland function. Because ACTH can activate several subtypes, its effects extend beyond the adrenals. The receptor family explains both ACTH's main job and its side effects.

Plain EnglishMelanocortin receptors are a family of five docking ports that respond to ACTH and its chemical cousins. The adrenal one, MC2R, is the key that starts cortisol production — and it answers only to ACTH. The others do different jobs: one controls skin and hair color, another helps regulate appetite, and the rest handle inflammation and glands. Because ACTH can plug into several of these ports, its effects reach beyond the adrenal glands. The family explains both what ACTH is for and why it has side effects elsewhere.

The scienceA synthetic fragment consisting of the first 24 amino acids of ACTH (ACTH 1-24), which contains the full sequence needed to activate the adrenal MC2R receptor. It is the standard agent for the ACTH stimulation test: administered, then blood cortisol is measured to check whether the adrenals respond normally, diagnosing adrenal insufficiency or distinguishing its causes. Because it is shorter and simpler than full-length ACTH, it is easier to manufacture and standardize. It is a diagnostic tool, not a treatment for the conditions ACTH therapy addresses. The test is one of endocrinology's most routine procedures.

Plain EnglishCosyntropin is ACTH's stunt double for testing — just the first 24 of its 39 building blocks, which is all the adrenal receptor needs to respond. Doctors give it and then measure cortisol in the blood to see if the adrenal glands are doing their job. If cortisol fails to rise, something is wrong with the adrenals or the signal reaching them. Being shorter makes it easier to make and standardize than the full hormone. It is a diagnostic tool, not a treatment — the medical equivalent of tapping the microphone to check it works.

The scienceA disorder in which the adrenal glands produce too little cortisol, and often too little aldosterone as well. Primary insufficiency (Addison's disease) means the glands themselves have failed, usually from autoimmune attack; secondary insufficiency means the pituitary is not sending enough ACTH; tertiary means the hypothalamus is not sending CRH. Symptoms include profound fatigue, weight loss, low blood pressure, and salt craving, and an adrenal crisis — triggered by illness or stress — can be fatal without prompt steroid treatment. Diagnosis typically involves the cosyntropin stimulation test. It is the condition ACTH signaling is most directly tied to.

Plain EnglishAdrenal insufficiency means the adrenal glands cannot make enough cortisol — the factory is not producing. Sometimes the glands themselves have failed, sometimes the pituitary is not sending the ACTH order, sometimes the hypothalamus above it is quiet. The result is crushing fatigue, weight loss, low blood pressure, and salt cravings — and during illness or stress, a sudden crisis that can be fatal without fast treatment. Doctors diagnose it with the cosyntropin stimulation test. It is the disease most directly tied to the ACTH signal going missing.

The scienceA rare but severe epilepsy syndrome of infancy, usually beginning before age one, marked by brief clusters of sudden muscle spasms and a chaotic brain-wave pattern called hypsarrhythmia. Without prompt treatment it can derail brain development, so it is considered a neurological emergency. ACTH (as Acthar Gel) is a first-line therapy: it suppresses the spasms in a substantial proportion of infants, through mechanisms that remain incompletely understood. Vigabatrin is the main alternative, especially when tuberous sclerosis is the cause. Early treatment decisively improves developmental outcomes, which is why rapid diagnosis matters.

Plain EnglishInfantile spasms are a rare, serious seizure disorder striking babies, usually in the first year — sudden clusters of jerking spasms paired with chaotic brain waves. Left untreated, they can derail a baby's brain development, so doctors treat it as an emergency. ACTH is one of the two first-choice treatments and stops the spasms in many infants, though exactly how it does this is still not fully understood. Speed matters enormously: the faster treatment starts, the better the child's development. It is one of ACTH's most important and most dramatic medical uses.

The scienceA long-acting drug formulation in which the active ingredient is suspended in a gel base — in Acthar Gel's case, a mixture including gelatin — so that a single injection releases the medicine slowly over hours to days. The repository (depot) design smooths out blood levels, avoiding the peaks and troughs of immediate-release dosing and reducing how often injections are needed. Acthar Gel contains a highly purified ACTH preparation in such a gel, which is central to its branding and its prolonged effect. The formulation is part of what distinguishes the product from plain ACTH. It is a delivery technology, not a different active drug.

Plain EnglishA repository gel is a time-release trick: the drug is suspended in a gel so one injection keeps releasing medicine slowly over hours or days. That smooths out the drug's levels in the blood and means fewer injections than an immediate-release version would need. Acthar Gel is exactly this — purified ACTH in a gelatin-based slow-release formula, which is a big part of the product's identity. The gel is delivery technology, not a different drug. Think of it as the difference between sipping coffee all morning and downing an espresso.

The scienceIn the HPA axis, the negative feedback loop by which rising cortisol levels signal the hypothalamus and pituitary to reduce CRH and ACTH output. This self-regulation keeps cortisol within a healthy range automatically: when levels climb, production throttles back; when they fall, the brakes come off. The loop also explains why long-term steroid drugs suppress the body's own cortisol production — the brain reads the medication as "enough" and stops ordering more. Disruption of feedback underlies Cushing's syndrome, where the loop fails to restrain excess cortisol. It is the thermostat of the stress system.

Plain EnglishThe feedback loop is the stress system's thermostat: when cortisol rises, it tells the brain to stop sending ACTH, and production eases off automatically. When cortisol falls, the brakes release and the signal resumes. This self-regulation keeps the hormone in a healthy range without any conscious effort. It also explains why long-term steroid drugs shut down the body's own production — the brain mistakes the medication for "enough" and stops ordering more. Like any thermostat, the trouble starts when it stops responding.

The scienceThe dampening of immune system activity — a predictable consequence of sustained high cortisol, whether from ACTH therapy, Cushing's syndrome, or steroid drugs. Cortisol suppresses inflammation by reducing the production of inflammatory signaling molecules and the activity of immune cells, which is therapeutically useful in autoimmune disease but leaves patients more vulnerable to infections. During ACTH treatment, this trade-off is monitored: benefit against the underlying disease weighed against infection risk. The effect reverses as cortisol levels normalize. It is the same mechanism that makes stress worsen susceptibility to colds.

Plain EnglishImmunosuppression means turning the immune system's volume down — and sustained high cortisol does exactly that. Cortisol quiets inflammation by dialing back immune cells and their chemical alarms, which helps in autoimmune disease but leaves the door open to infections. During ACTH therapy, doctors watch this trade-off closely: the benefit against the disease versus the infection risk. The effect fades as cortisol returns to normal. It is the same reason a brutal stretch of stress can end with you catching every cold going around.

The scienceA kidney disorder defined by heavy protein loss in the urine (proteinuria), low blood protein, swelling (edema), and high cholesterol. It results from damage to the kidney's filtering units, the glomeruli, which begin leaking protein that should stay in the blood. Acthar Gel carries an FDA-approved indication for inducing remission of proteinuria in certain forms of nephrotic syndrome, offering an option when standard immunosuppressive therapy fails or is unsuitable. The mechanism is not fully established — proposed effects include direct action on kidney cells via melanocortin receptors. It remains a specialist-prescribed therapy, not a first-line treatment.

Plain EnglishNephrotic syndrome is a kidney disorder where the blood filters start leaking protein into the urine — bringing swelling, low blood protein, and high cholesterol with it. Acthar Gel is officially approved to help push certain forms into remission, giving doctors an option when standard immune-suppressing drugs fail or cannot be used. How it helps the kidneys is still not fully worked out; one theory involves ACTH acting directly on kidney cells. It is a specialist's tool, not a first-choice treatment. But for some patients, it is the option that works when others do not.
